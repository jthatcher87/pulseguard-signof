Executive Overview & Product Core Definition

The Core Architectural Problem

Traditional public health contact tracing and partner notification systems for sexually transmitted infections (STIs) fail due to four systemic market gaps:

1. The "Contact Info Gap": In anonymous, casual, or nightlife environments (e.g., festivals, private gatherings, bathhouses, sex-positive venues), participants rarely exchange personal contact details, legal names, or phone numbers. If an infection is diagnosed weeks later, notifying partners is impossible under conventional methods.

2. Cognitive Recall & Diagnostic Window Failure: Human memory is fallible; individuals forget dates, miscalculate variable pathogen incubation periods, or selectively omit partners due to substance use or anxiety.

3. Social Stigma & Confrontation: Direct personal disclosure carries severe emotional friction, fear of social ostracization, and potential interpersonal conflict, causing people to delay or skip partner notification.

4. Venue & Host Liability Trap: Organizers of sex-positive events and nightlife venues face liability and community panic if they attempt to notify attendees via unsegmented broadcast lists or guest registries.

Target Cohorts & Phased Rollout

PulseGuard addresses high-density social environments through a structured three-wave adoption strategy:

  • Cohort 1 — LGBTQ+ & Queer Communities (Initial Wedge): High baseline health literacy, proactive routine testing cadences (e.g., quarterly PrEP and Doxy-PEP monitoring), and established community-care norms.
  • Cohort 2 — Sex-Positive, Kink & Lifestyle Networks: Communities with formalized consent frameworks, vetting protocols, and an urgent demand for zero-judgment, collective harm-reduction infrastructure.
  • Cohort 3 — Colleges & University Campuses: High-density social environments seeking discreet, confidential sexual wellness tools free from campus surveillance.

    Technical Architecture & On-Device Security Stack

    Core Framework & Hardware Isolation

  • Cross-Platform Mobile Client: Built on Flutter / Dart implementing Material 3 design tokens and reactive state management (Riverpod / Bloc).
  • On-Device Encrypted Storage: Utilizes SQLite compiled with SQLCipher in Write-Ahead Logging (WAL) mode, encrypted with AES-GCM-256.
  • Hardware-Backed Key Derivation: Cryptographic master keys are derived directly from the Apple Secure Enclave Processor (SEP) on iOS and Android StrongBox / Keymaster on Android. Master keys are never written to flash storage or exposed to the application runtime.

    • Dual-Enclave Ledger Separation (Zero Foreign Keys)

    To neutralize relational database introspection attacks or memory-dump forensics:

  • Ledger A (Clinical History Vault): Encrypted with hardware key $K_{\text{health}}$. Stores verified LOINC diagnostic codes, qualitative test results, specimen collection dates, and detached lab signatures. It contains zero references to encounter tokens, partner identifiers, or geolocation.
  • Ledger B (Ephemeral Encounter Vault): Encrypted with hardware key $K_{\text{tokens}}$. Stores dynamic check-in tokens, timestamps, duration bounds, preventative regimens, and anatomical transmission vectors. It contains zero references to medical diagnoses or user identity.
  • Zero Foreign Keys: There are no foreign keys, relational indices, or join tables linking Ledger A to Ledger B. Correlation between an infection window and past encounter tokens occurs exclusively inside volatile RAM during risk evaluation and is purged immediately.

    • Standards-Based Healthcare Integration

  • SMART on FHIR (Epic Systems, Cerner/Oracle, Athenahealth): Connects via OAuth 2.0 / OpenID Connect within secure system webviews (ASWebAuthenticationSession on iOS, Custom Tabs on Android), querying standardized HL7 FHIR Observation and DiagnosticReport resources.
  • SMART Health Cards (Quest Diagnostics, LabCorp, State Health Registries): Ingests digital verifiable credentials formatted as compact JSON Web Signatures (JWS) via scanned 2D QR codes or .smart-health-card files, verified 100% offline using public keys from the official VCI registry.
  • Aggregator Bridges: Interfaces directly with consumer health bridges (Health Gorilla, Particle Health, 1upHealth, and Apple HealthKit).

    • Sub-10ms Volatile RAM Surgical PII Shredder

    1. Direct-to-Device TLS: Client network requests route directly between the mobile device and the diagnostic provider's API. PulseGuard corporate servers never touch or proxy the session.

    2. In-Memory Triage & Verification: The raw FHIR payload arrives in volatile RAM. The signature is verified against the lab's public key, and non-identifying attributes are extracted: LOINC code, diagnostic result (Positive), and specimen collection timestamp.

    3. RAM Zero-Out Protocol: All identifying data (Patient.name, Patient.birthDate, Patient.telecom, Patient.address, MRN, Provider.name, and the OAuth access token) are overwritten with binary zeros (memset_s) and deallocated in $<10\text{ ms}$.

    4. Persistent Isolation: Only non-identifying diagnostic attributes and the detached signature are committed to Ledger A. Zero Protected Health Information (PHI) is ever written to disk.

    Rotating Beacons & Handshakes

  • Ephemeral Event Master Seeds (EMS): In group/venue settings, the host creates an in-memory master seed valid only for the event duration (4–8 hours).
  • 30-Second Rotating HMAC Beacons: The host screen displays a dynamic QR code rotating every 30 seconds: $$\text{Token}_t = \text{HMAC-SHA256}(EMS, \lfloor \text{Timestamp}_t / 30 \rfloor)$$
  • Zero Host Logging: The host device stores zero attendee tokens, logs no device IDs, and keeps no guest list. Attendees derive and store their own tokens in Ledger B.

    • Detached JWS Laboratory Signatures

  • The Verification Paradox: How to prove a test result is authentic without linking the patient's identity to the broadcast.
  • Detached Signature Protocol (RFC 7515): The client strips the identifying patient block from the lab's SMART Health Card and retains only the cryptographic assertion: $$\text{Signature} = \text{Sign}{K{\text{lab}}}(\text{Digest}(\text{LOINC} \mathbin{\Vert} \text{Result} \mathbin{\Vert} \text{Date}))$$
  • The public relay verifies the laboratory's ECDSA ES256 public key against the VCI trust registry, issuing an immutable 🛡️ Lab-Verified Clinical Notice trust badge with mathematical certainty and zero knowledge of user identity.

    • Stateless Relays & Anonymous Transport

  • BLAKE3 Hash Manifests: The client hashes all encounter tokens falling within the clinical lookback window using BLAKE3: $$\text{Manifest} = { \text{BLAKE3}(\text{Token}_1), \text{BLAKE3}(\text{Token}_2), \dots, \text{Pathogen_Code}, \text{Detached_Signature} }$$
  • OHTTP & Tor Relays: Requests route through Oblivious HTTP (OHTTP) or Tor proxies, stripping client IP addresses, device user-agents, and ISP metadata.
  • Traffic Obfuscation: The client introduces random transmission jitter (1–4 hours) and injects dummy (chaff) packets, neutralizing traffic-correlation attacks.

    • Local-Only Blind Matching

  • Stateless Broadcast Bulletin: The central relay functions as a stateless, append-only message board.
  • Client-Side Private Intersection: All client devices periodically fetch newly published manifest hashes in the background. Comparison between downloaded hashes and local encounter tokens occurs entirely on the user's phone inside Ledger B.
  • Zero-Knowledge Alerting: The central server is never informed when a match occurs. If a match is detected locally, the OS issues an ambient notification ("There is new health data to review"), requiring biometric unlock to inspect the clinical advisory.

  • Generative Data Cloak: Translates diagnostic states and transmission windows into evolving, ambient particle physics visualizations, eliminating overt medical terminology from default screens.
